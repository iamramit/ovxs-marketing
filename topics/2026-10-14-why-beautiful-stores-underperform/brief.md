### So what
- Shoppers rarely leave because a store looks bad. Baymard ties most fixable losses to checkout friction, worth up to a 35% conversion gain.
- Trust builds quickly. The first few reviews on a product do most of the work, so "no reviews" is a cheap problem to fix.
- Founders with "classy" stores and no sales keep getting told to look at the funnel before the theme. That's exactly the gap the audit fills.

## Angles

### A — Business first, design second (your original angle)
- Hook: "A beautiful store is not a working store."
- Argument: Hire people who understand checkout, pricing, speed and trust. Design is the last 20%.
- Proof used: Baymard's 35% checkout gain; Halm's 400+ reviews.
- Fits: Honest advice · Everyone
- Risk: Every agency claims to be "more than design". The "last 20%" line is our opinion, so it has to read as one.

### B — The six-check afternoon
- Hook: "Before you pay for a redesign, spend one afternoon on these six checks."
- Argument: Most stalled stores can find their own problem for free. Give them the list, and offer the audit if they get stuck.
- Proof used: 70% cart abandonment; 1 in 5 leave over a long checkout; form fields can drop 20–60%.
- Fits: Shopify know-how · Growing stores
- Risk: Checklists are everywhere. It needs one sharp OVXS observation per check to stand out.

### C — Don't redesign (yet)
- Hook: "The most expensive fix for a slow store is a redesign. It's usually the wrong one."
- Argument: Founders reach for a new theme when sales stall. The cheap fixes come first: reviews, checkout, apps. We'd rather say that than sell a redesign.
- Proof used: the first 5 reviews drive most of the lift (Spiegel, 2017); Baymard's 64% of checkouts are mediocre or worse.
- Fits: Honest advice · Growing stores
- Risk: Can read as anti-design from a studio that sells design. Needs one line on when a redesign is the right call.
