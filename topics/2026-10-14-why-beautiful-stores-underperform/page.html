Shopify store not converting? Six checks before you redesign

One afternoon, no budget, and a straight list. Run it before anyone sells you a new theme.

A version of the same message reaches us most weeks. The store has been live for a few months. Friends say it looks great. Traffic is coming in. Sales aren’t. And the last line is nearly always the same: “We’re thinking about a redesign.”

If your Shopify store is not converting, a redesign is the most expensive thing you can try, and it’s rarely the first thing that’s wrong. Most of the time the problem is cheaper than that, and you can find it yourself in one afternoon. Here are the six checks we’d run first. Do them in order, with a notebook, on your phone.

1. The market: why you, and not the first result?

Search the main thing you sell, the way a shopper would. Open the first three results. Note their price, their promise and how fast they deliver.

Now write one line: why would someone buy this from you rather than from them? If that line is hard to write, the shopper is having the same trouble. No theme fixes it. The fix is on the product page, in plain words, above the fold.

2. Apps: what did each one earn last month?

Open your installed apps and list them. Next to each one, write what it did for sales last month. Not what it promised — what it did.

We often find two apps doing the same job, or an app doing something the theme already does. Each one adds scripts, weight and another thing that can break. Remove the blanks. A lighter store is a faster store, and you don’t need us to do it.

3. Checkout: buy from your own store

Buy one of your own products, on your phone, on mobile data. Count the taps from the product page to “Paid”. Count the form fields. Note every surprise, especially shipping costs that appear late.

This is where the numbers are clearest. The average documented cart abandonment rate is 70.22%, and about 1 in 5 shoppers say they’ve abandoned an order because checkout was too long or complicated (Baymard Institute, 2025). Baymard also finds that 64% of sites have checkout UX that is “mediocre” or worse (2025). Shopify’s checkout is already good. What usually hurts is what’s added around it: forced accounts, extra steps, late costs.

4. Pricing: put yours next to three others

Take the three competitors from check one and put their prices next to yours, including shipping.

Being more expensive is fine. Saying nothing about it isn’t. If you cost more, the page needs to say why — materials, service, guarantees, who makes it. If you can’t say why, that’s a pricing question, not a design one.

5. Order value: what appears after “Add to cart”?

Add something to your cart and watch what happens next. Is there a sensible second item? A bundle? A note on the free shipping threshold?

Getting a visitor to your store costs money. Getting a little more from each visitor costs very little. If the answer to “what appears next” is nothing, that’s a sale you didn’t ask for. Keep it to one relevant suggestion. Three pop-ups are worse than none.

6. Trust: would you buy from you?

Open a product page as if you’d never heard of the brand. Look for reviews, a clear returns policy, a real way to contact someone, and a social account that posted something this month.

A product page with no reviews is asking a stranger to go first. Reviews are one of the cheapest problems to fix, and they build on each other. For Halm the store runs on a subscription and a steady flow of customer feedback: a 4.8 star average rating, 400+ customer reviews and 3 seasonal deliveries a year on subscription.

Business first, then design

Notice what isn’t on the list: fonts, colours, the hero image. Design matters, and we do a lot of it. But in our experience it’s the last 20%. A plain store that gets these six right will usually outsell a beautiful one that doesn’t.

So before you brief anyone for a redesign, give the six checks an afternoon. You may find the problem yourself. If you do, fix it, and keep your budget.
