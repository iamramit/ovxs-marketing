- The gap: a senior team saying plainly that a good-looking store can still be hard to buy from, naming the six leaks in shoppers’ terms, and saying most fixes are small — no new app, no redesign.

### So what
- Even the biggest stores get the basics wrong: about two in three leading sites are mediocre or worse on mobile navigation and product pages (Baymard, 2025).
- Roughly 7 in 10 carts are abandoned (Baymard, 2025). Looks bring shoppers in; navigation, product detail, trust and a clear next step decide whether they buy.
- Most of these leaks are small, specific fixes, not a redesign or another app — which is exactly what the $99 audit ranks.

## Angles
### A — Design gets attention. UX gets the sale. (Ramit’s angle, sharpened)
- Hook: Your store can look great and still lose customers.
- Argument: Good design earns the click; six plain UX leaks — confusing navigation, thin product info, clumsy mobile, missing trust, friction, an unclear next step — decide whether anyone buys.
- Proof used: Baymard mobile navigation (67%) and product page (62% mobile) benchmarks; 70.22% cart abandonment; $99 audit’s three ranked fixes.
- Fits: Honest advice · Everyone (growing stores)
- Risk: Close to “Why beautiful stores still underperform”. Keep this one about the six buying leaks and Ramit’s closing lines, not design vs business.

### B — Follow one shopper on a phone
- Hook: A shopper taps your ad on their phone. Here are the six places they quietly leave.
- Argument: Walk one shopper from landing to checkout and show each leak as a moment — the menu they can’t find, the size they can’t check, the button below the fold.
- Proof used: Baymard mobile navigation (67%) and mobile product page (62%) findings; 70.22% abandonment.
- Fits: Honest advice · Growing stores
- Risk: The shopper must be a clearly illustrative composite, never a client or a real customer.

### C — Six questions every shopper asks before buying
- Hook: Every shopper asks six questions before they buy. A pretty store can answer none of them.
- Argument: Reframe each leak as the shopper’s question — Where is it? What exactly am I getting? Does this work on my phone? Can I trust you? Is this easy? What do I do next? — and show what a good answer looks like.
- Proof used: Baymard product description and product page findings; cart abandonment rate; custom section from $250 for a single fix.
- Fits: Honest advice · Everyone
- Risk: A six-point list sits near Brief 1’s “six-check afternoon”. The question framing must carry it, with the checklist kept short.
