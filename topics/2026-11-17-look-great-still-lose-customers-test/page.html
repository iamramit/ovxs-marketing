Shopify store not converting? Six questions your shoppers need answered

A store can look great and still lose customers. Here’s what shoppers quietly ask before they buy, and what a good answer looks like.

It’s late in the evening. Someone taps your ad on their phone, half-watching something else. Your homepage loads and the photos are lovely. They scroll, open the menu, can’t see what they came for, and go back to their programme. Nobody tells you. In your analytics, it’s one more visit that didn’t convert.

If your Shopify store is not converting, this is usually how it happens. Not one big failure, but a few small questions nobody answered.

We see it often in our audits: a store that looks good, gets steady traffic and still doesn’t sell enough. The owner tends to blame the traffic, the ads or the season. In our experience, the store simply didn’t answer what the shopper was asking.

Every shopper asks six questions before they buy. Here they are, with what a good answer looks like.

1. “Where is it?”

Shoppers arrive with something in mind. If the menu is organised the way you stock products, with collection names, internal ranges or clever labels, they have to translate. Most won’t bother.

A good answer: menu labels in the words shoppers search with, no more than one or two levels deep on a phone, and a search bar people can see without hunting for an icon.

2. “What exactly am I getting?”

In a shop, people pick a product up. Online, the product page has to do that job, and many don’t. Baymard Institute’s product page benchmark rates 62% of mobile sites and 52% of desktop sites as mediocre or worse (Baymard). Usually the basics are missing: size, materials, what’s in the box, when it arrives.

A good answer: the details people need to decide, close to the add to cart button. Delivery time and returns in one line, not on a separate page. Photos that show scale and use, not only the product on white.

3. “Does this work on my phone?”

Check your own analytics, but for many stores most visits now come from phones, and that’s where small problems get big. A pop-up covering the page. Buttons too small for a thumb. A sticky bar that eats a third of the screen. A menu that takes four taps.

A good answer: try your store on your own phone, one-handed, on mobile data, and buy something. Every moment you hesitate is a moment a shopper leaves.

4. “Can I trust you?”

A new shopper doesn’t know you. In Baymard’s research, 19% of shoppers who abandoned a checkout said they didn’t trust the site with their card details (Baymard).

A good answer: real reviews on the product page, a returns policy that’s easy to find and easy to read, a way to reach a person, and a checkout that looks like a checkout. Badges from yet another app rarely do this; real proof does. Halm, one of our case studies, has a 4.8 star average rating from 400+ customer reviews. That kind of proof works harder than any badge.

5. “Is this easy?”

Friction is anything that makes buying harder than it needs to be. In Baymard’s data, the most common reason for leaving a checkout is extra costs that are too high, at 40%. Being asked to create an account comes in at 18% (Baymard).

A good answer: shipping costs shown before checkout, guest checkout switched on, and as few steps and fields as possible. Shopify’s checkout is good out of the box. In our experience, the problems come from what gets added around it.

6. “What do I do next?”

Every page should have one obvious next step. When a homepage offers a slideshow, three banners, a newsletter pop-up and a quiz, the shopper has to choose, and choosing is work.

A good answer: one main button per page, worded for what happens next, like “Add to cart” or “Shop the range”, with everything else visually quieter.

Good design gets attention. Good UX makes buying easier.

None of this is an argument against design. A good-looking store earns the first few seconds, and that matters. But looks bring shoppers in; answers make the sale.

Roughly 7 in 10 online carts are abandoned (Baymard). No store gets that to zero, but each of the six questions is a place to win some back.

The good news is that most of these are small fixes, not a redesign. A clearer menu. A fuller product page. One less step at checkout. Sometimes it’s a single custom section on a product page, which we build from $250.
